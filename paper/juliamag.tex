\begin{abstract}
We present \JuliaMag, an open-source micromagnetic simulation package written
entirely in the Julia programming language. \JuliaMag{} solves the
Landau--Lifshitz--Gilbert (LLG) equation on a finite-difference mesh, including
the exchange, magnetostatic (demagnetizing), uniaxial anisotropy, Zeeman, and
Dzyaloshinskii--Moriya interactions, as well as the Zhang--Li and Slonczewski
spin-transfer torques. The code is designed around Julia's multiple dispatch:
every field and torque operates on an abstract array type, so the same source
runs unchanged on CPU and GPU arrays, and material parameters are read through
accessors so that a single-material and a multi-region (multilayer) simulation
share one implementation with no overhead in the uniform case. We describe the
data layout, the field and torque implementations, the FFT-accelerated
demagnetization convolution, the time integration and energy minimization, the
geometry/region model, the output and simulation interface, and a CUDA package
extension that adds GPU methods additively through dispatch. We validate
\JuliaMag{} against the \mumax{} and \oommf{} reference codes on the $\mu$MAG
standard problems~2, 4, and~5 --- covering static equilibria and the
demagnetizing field, dynamic magnetization reversal, and current-driven
spin-transfer torque --- reproducing the references to within
$\sim 4\times10^{-3}$ in the spatially averaged magnetization. On a vortex in a
Permalloy disc driven by a spin-polarized current, run on the GPU, the
demagnetizing and exchange fields agree with \mumax{} cell by cell to
$10^{-6}$ and the vortex gyration frequency to better than $10^{-4}$ over
$40$~ns, a test that exercises the treatment of curved boundaries and the
Slonczewski torque.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Micromagnetics is the continuum theory of ferromagnetic materials at the
sub-micron scale, in which the magnetization is treated as a continuous
vector field whose evolution is governed by the Landau--Lifshitz--Gilbert (LLG)
equation~\cite{miltat2007,abert2019}. Because the effective field entering the
LLG equation couples every part of the sample through the long-ranged
magnetostatic interaction, and because the exchange interaction imposes a short
length scale, analytical solutions exist only for the simplest geometries.
Numerical micromagnetics has therefore become an indispensable tool, from the
earliest finite-difference computations of domain-wall structure~\cite{labonte1969}
and the stochastic foundations of thermally activated
switching~\cite{brown1963} to today's routine simulation of spintronic devices,
skyrmions, and domain-wall memories.

Two finite-difference codes dominate the field. \oommf~\cite{oommf}, developed at
NIST, has been the community reference for over two decades. \mumax~\cite{mumax3}
brought micromagnetics to the graphics processing unit (GPU), achieving order-of-
magnitude speedups~\cite{lopezdiaz2012} and enabling simulations that were
previously impractical. Both are highly optimized, but both are also large,
performance-tuned codebases --- \oommf{} in C\texttt{++} with a Tcl interface,
\mumax{} in Go with hand-written CUDA kernels --- in which the numerical methods
are interwoven with low-level implementation detail. For a researcher who wishes
to read, modify, or extend the core algorithms, or to prototype a new physical
term or solver, this coupling is a barrier.

The central computational cost in both codes is the magnetostatic
(demagnetizing) field, a dense convolution of the magnetization with a geometric
kernel. Evaluated directly it scales as $O(N^2)$ in the number of cells; the
standard remedy, adopted here, is the fast-Fourier-transform convolution of
Hayashi \textit{et al.}~\cite{hayashi1996}, which reduces the cost to
$O(N\log N)$ and remains the workhorse of finite-difference
micromagnetics~\cite{lepadatu2019}.

We present \JuliaMag, a micromagnetic solver written entirely in the Julia
programming language~\cite{julia}. Our aim is not to outperform the established
GPU codes but to provide a \emph{transparent} and \emph{extensible} reference
implementation in which the numerical methods are expressed directly, at the
level of the mathematics, and are validated against \oommf{} and \mumax{} to high
accuracy. Julia is well suited to this goal: it combines a high-level,
mathematical syntax with performance approaching that of C, and its multiple
dispatch lets a single generic implementation of each field and torque serve both
CPU and GPU arrays without duplication. The scientific Julia ecosystem, in
particular the \texttt{DifferentialEquations.jl} suite~\cite{diffeq}, further
supplies community-validated adaptive time integrators, which \JuliaMag{} offers
alongside its own step-for-step ports of the \mumax{} solvers.

The remainder of the paper describes the theory (Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}), the
architecture and data layout (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}), the effective-field terms and
the FFT-accelerated demagnetization (Sec.~\ref{sec:fields}), the torques
including spin-transfer (Sec.~\ref{sec:torques}), the time integration and energy
minimization (Sec.~\ref{sec:solvers}), the initial states and I/O
(Sec.~\ref{sec:config}), the geometry and material-region model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:regions}), the output and simulation interface
(Sec.~\ref{sec:interface}), and the GPU package extension
(Sec.~\ref{sec:gpu}). Section~\ref{sec:validation} validates \JuliaMag{}
against the \mumax{} and \oommf{} reference codes on the $\mu$MAG standard
problems~2, 4, and~5, which together exercise the static equilibria and
demagnetizing field, the dynamic magnetization reversal, and the spin-transfer
torque, respectively, and on a current-driven vortex in a disc, which tests the
curved-boundary treatment and the Slonczewski torque on the GPU.

\section{Theory}
\label{sec:theory}

The state of a micromagnetic system is the reduced magnetization field
$\vm(\bm{r},t) = \bm{M}(\bm{r},t)/M_s$, a unit-vector field with $M_s$ the
saturation magnetization. Its dynamics obey the Landau--Lifshitz--Gilbert (LLG)
equation, written here in the explicit (Landau--Lifshitz) form
%
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial \vm}{\partial t}
  = -\frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^2}
    \Big[\, \vm\times\vB_{\mathrm{eff}}
          + \alpha\,\vm\times(\vm\times\vB_{\mathrm{eff}}) \,\Big],
  \label{eq:llg}
\end{equation}
%
where $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio and $\alpha$ the Gilbert damping. The
effective field $\vB_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the variational derivative of the total
energy density $\varepsilon$ with respect to the magnetization,
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{eff}} = -\frac{1}{M_s}\frac{\delta \varepsilon}{\delta \vm},
  \label{eq:beff}
\end{equation}
%
a genuine field in tesla. It is the sum of the contributing terms,
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{eff}}
  = \vB_{\mathrm{exch}} + \vB_{\mathrm{demag}}
  + \vB_{\mathrm{anis}} + \vB_{\mathrm{DMI}} + \vB_{\mathrm{ext}},
  \label{eq:beffsum}
\end{equation}
%
each defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:fields}. Two current-driven spin-transfer torques,
of the Zhang--Li and Slonczewski types, are added to the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:llg} when a current is present (Sec.~\ref{sec:stt}).

We follow the convention of \mumax{}~\cite{mumax3} and \oommf{}~\cite{oommf}
throughout: the effective field carries no factor of $\mu_0$ in the exchange,
anisotropy, or DMI terms [Eq.~\eqref{eq:beff}]; $\mu_0$ appears only in the
demagnetizing and Zeeman fields, which are physical $\bm{B}$ fields.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

\subsection{Design goals}

\JuliaMag{} is written to be \emph{minimal}, \emph{transparent}, and
\emph{portable to the GPU without a rewrite}. These goals are met by three
decisions that pervade the code.

\paragraph{Data layout.}
The magnetization is stored as a dense four-dimensional array of shape
$(3, N_x, N_y, N_z)$, with the Cartesian component as the fastest-varying index:
%
\begin{equation}
  \vm \;\longleftrightarrow\; \texttt{m::Array\{T,4\}}, \quad
  \vm(i,j,k) = \texttt{m[:,\,i,\,j,\,k]}.
\end{equation}
%
Placing the component first makes each cell's vector contiguous in memory, which
vectorizes the per-cell cross products and, crucially, lets the whole array map
onto a GPU \texttt{CuArray} without any restructuring. The element type \texttt{T}
is a type parameter, so a simulation runs in \texttt{Float64} or in
\texttt{Float32}, on the CPU or on the GPU, with no change to the source.

\paragraph{Multiple dispatch.}
Every field and torque routine is written against the abstract signature
\texttt{AbstractArray\{T,4\}}:
%
\begin{lstlisting}
function exchange!(B::AbstractArray{T,4},
                   m::AbstractArray{T,4},
                   mesh, mat; add=false) where {T}
    # ... generic stencil ...
end
\end{lstlisting}
%
CPU execution passes an \texttt{Array}; GPU execution will pass a \texttt{CuArray}
and Julia dispatches to a device method with the same name. There are no
\texttt{if gpu} branches and no duplicated call sites; GPU support is additive.

\paragraph{Index convention.}
All routines use a single $(x,y,z)$, one-based index convention, and the
coordinate origin is placed at the geometric centre of the sample, so cell
centres run from $-L/2$ to $+L/2$ along each axis. This matches \mumax{} and lets
localized initial states (vortices, skyrmions) be positioned by translation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:config}).

\subsection{Package structure}

The package is organized as one module per physical concept:
\texttt{Mesh} and \texttt{Material} hold the geometry and parameters;
\texttt{magnetization}/\texttt{config} build states; \texttt{exchange},
\texttt{anisotropy}, \texttt{dmi}, \texttt{zeeman}, \texttt{demag\_kernel},
and \texttt{demag\_field} implement the effective-field terms;
\texttt{effective\_field} assembles them; \texttt{llg} and \texttt{stt} form the
torques; \texttt{solver}, \texttt{integrator}, and \texttt{minimizer} advance or
relax the state; \texttt{energy} computes energies; and \texttt{ovf} reads the
standard interchange format. The \texttt{World} type bundles a mesh, a material,
a cached demagnetization plan, and the applied field, and is the single object
passed to the dynamics.

\section{Effective-field terms}
\label{sec:fields}

\subsection{Exchange}

In the continuum limit the Heisenberg exchange field is
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{exch}} = \frac{2A}{M_s}\,\nabla^2\vm,
  \label{eq:exch}
\end{equation}
%
with $A$ the exchange stiffness. The Laplacian is discretized with the standard
six-neighbour stencil,
$\nabla^2\vm|_c \approx \sum_{\text{axes}}(\vm_{-}+\vm_{+}-2\vm_c)/\Delta^2$.
At a free boundary a Neumann condition $\partial\vm/\partial n = 0$ is imposed by
taking the missing neighbour equal to the central cell, so its contribution
vanishes; across a periodic axis the index wraps around. We verified
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exch} against the analytic spin-wave field
$-\frac{2A}{M_s}q^2\vm$ for $\vm=(\sin qx,0,\cos qx)$ to a relative error of
$10^{-2}$ in the mesh interior.

\subsection{Uniaxial anisotropy}

For a uniaxial easy axis $\bm{u}$ and anisotropy constant $K_u$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{anis}} = \frac{2K_u}{M_s}(\vm\cdot\bm{u})\,\bm{u}.
  \label{eq:anis}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Dzyaloshinskii--Moriya interaction}

Two DMI forms are provided. The interfacial (N\'eel) DMI, for thin films with
broken inversion symmetry, has field
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{DMI}}^{\mathrm{int}}
  = \frac{2D}{M_s}
    \Big(\partial_x m_z,\ \partial_y m_z,\ -\partial_x m_x-\partial_y m_y\Big),
\end{equation}
%
with $D$ in J/m$^2$. The bulk (Bloch) DMI, for chiral B20 crystals, has
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{DMI}}^{\mathrm{bulk}} = -\frac{2D}{M_s}\,\nabla\times\vm,
\end{equation}
%
with $D$ in J/m$^2$ as well. Gradients use central differences, periodic where the
mesh is. At free boundaries (the edge of an open axis or an empty cell) the chiral
boundary condition of the DMI~\cite{rohart2013} is applied as in \mumax{}. There the
missing neighbour is extrapolated with $\partial_n\vm$ from that condition in both
the exchange and the DMI stencils. The terms linear in $D$ cancel, and each missing
neighbour adds $(D^2/2AM_s)\,\mathsf{P}\vm$ to the exchange field with a Neumann
boundary, where $\mathsf{P}$ keeps the two components that the DMI couples along that
axis. The open boundary of \mumax{} (\texttt{OpenBC}) is available too. Where $D$
differs between two regions, their bond takes the mean of \mumax{}: harmonic if the
signs agree, and zero if either $D$ is zero. The edge term is diagonal in the cells and
the bonds are symmetric, so exchange plus DMI remains the exact gradient of the energy
when all cells are full. On random states with open, periodic and curved boundaries,
also edge-smoothed, and with one to three regions, the sum of the exchange and DMI
fields agrees with \mumax{} to $1.5\times10^{-7}$ of the largest field, its single
precision, and the energies to $1\times10^{-7}$ (28 cases). The edge tilt of a film with perpendicular
anisotropy, $\sin\theta=D/(2\sqrt{AK})=0.39$ for the parameters tested, and its
decay into the film~\cite{rohart2013} are reproduced to $8\times10^{-4}$,
$3\times10^{-4}$ and $9\times10^{-5}$~rad with 2, 1 and 0.5~nm cells. A Bloch helix
seeded at the equilibrium period $L = 4\pi A/D$ has a smaller residual torque than
one at the wrong period.

\subsection{Demagnetizing field: kernel and FFT convolution}
\label{sec:demag}

The magnetostatic (demagnetizing) field is a convolution of the magnetization
with a mesh-dependent tensor $\hat{N}$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \vB_{\mathrm{demag}}(\bm{r})
  = \mu_0 M_s \sum_{\bm{r}'} \hat{K}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}')\cdot\vm(\bm{r}'),
  \label{eq:demagconv}
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat{K}$ (our stored kernel, equal to $-\hat{N}$) is the field of a
unit-magnetized source cell. Because $\hat{K}$ depends only on the cell offset,
it is computed once per mesh by adaptive brute-force integration of the
surface-charge field over each destination cell (the \mumax{} method): a cell is
sub-sampled more finely the closer it is to the source, and the source and
destination sub-grids are staggered to improve accuracy. Only the six independent
components of the symmetric tensor are stored.

Evaluating Eq.~\eqref{eq:demagconv} directly is $O(N^2)$ in the number of cells.
The convolution theorem reduces it to $O(N\log N)$: the magnetization is
zero-padded to a size that turns the circular FFT convolution into the linear
convolution required, the kernel is transformed once at setup, and each timestep
performs a forward real FFT, a pointwise tensor--vector product in Fourier space,
and an inverse FFT. As in \mumax{}, the kernel's reflection symmetry is used to
halve the work: each diagonal component is even and each off-diagonal component
odd under the reflections of the offset, so after the odd components are set
exactly to zero on their symmetry planes the Fourier-space kernel is real and
only its real part is stored. The kernel is transformed in \texttt{Float64}
whatever the run precision, with the $1/N$ normalization of the inverse transform
folded in, and the three magnetization components are transformed in a single
batched call. The transformed kernel, the FFT plans, and the scratch
buffers are cached in a \texttt{DemagPlan} object built once per simulation. We
verified the FFT path against a direct real-space circular convolution with the
same kernel to a relative error below $10^{-12}$, an accuracy check independent of any
analytic approximation. The self-demagnetization factors of a cubic cell come out
to $1/3$ per axis and satisfy the trace sum rule $N_{xx}+N_{yy}+N_{zz}=1$.

\section{Torques}
\label{sec:torques}

\subsection{Landau--Lifshitz torque}

The LLG torque [Eq.~\eqref{eq:llg}] is evaluated per cell as two cross products,
$\bm{p}=\vm\times\vB_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $\bm{q}=\vm\times\bm{p}$, combined as
$-\frac{\gamma}{1+\alpha^2}(\bm{p}+\alpha\bm{q})$. Written over
\texttt{AbstractArray\{T,4\}}, the same routine serves CPU and GPU.

\subsection{Spin-transfer torques}
\label{sec:stt}

A spin-polarized current adds a torque to the right-hand side of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:llg}. \JuliaMag{} implements both standard forms, following the
\mumax{} kernels.

For an in-plane current through a magnetization texture, the Zhang--Li torque is
%
\begin{equation}
  \bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{ZL}}
  = \frac{-1}{1+\alpha^2}
    \Big[(1+\xi\alpha)\,\vm\times(\vm\times\bm{h}_s)
        + (\xi-\alpha)\,\vm\times\bm{h}_s\Big],
  \label{eq:zhangli}
\end{equation}
%
with $\bm{h}_s = (b\,\bm{J}\cdot\nabla)\vm$, $b = \mu_B/[e\,M_s(1+\xi^2)]$,
$\bm{J}=P\bm{j}$ the polarized current density, and $\xi$ the
non-adiabaticity; $bPj = P\mu_B j/[e M_s(1+\xi^2)]$ is the spin-drift
velocity $u$. Because \JuliaMag{} carries $\gamma$ inside the torque, $b$
contains no $1/\gamma$. \mumax{} writes the same term with the prefactor
$\mu_B/[2e\gamma M_s(1+\xi^2)]$ multiplying the \emph{undivided} central
difference $(\vm_{+}-\vm_{-})/\Delta = 2\,\partial\vm$; \JuliaMag{} reproduces this
arithmetic operation for operation, with Neumann/periodic boundaries.

For a perpendicular current in a spin valve, the Slonczewski torque is
%
\begin{align}
  \bm{\tau}_{\mathrm{SL}} &= g_\alpha\big[(A+\alpha B)\,\vm\times(\bm{p}\times\vm)
                             + (B-\alpha A)\,(\bm{p}\times\vm)\big], \\
  A &= \beta\,\epsilon,\quad B = \beta\,\epsilon',\quad
      g_\alpha = \frac{1}{1+\alpha^2}, \notag
\end{align}
%
with $\beta = (\hbar/e)\,j/(t\,M_s)$ (times $\gamma$ in our convention), fixed-layer
polarization direction $\bm{p}$, free-layer thickness $t$, angular efficiency
$\epsilon = P\Lambda^2/[(\Lambda^2+1)+(\Lambda^2-1)(\bm{p}\cdot\vm)]$, and the
secondary (field-like) term $\epsilon'$. As in \mumax, the torque is applied
uniformly across the free layer, whose thickness $t$ defaults to that of the
mesh. Unlike \mumax, which adds the Zhang--Li term as well whenever a current
density is set (unless \texttt{DisableZhangLiTorque} is enabled), \JuliaMag{}
adds each spin-transfer torque only when it is called explicitly; the
consequence of this difference is quantified in Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt}.

A subtlety worth recording: \mumax{} applies the gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma$ in
the time integrator (its step is $\gamma\,\Delta t$ times the torque), so its
torque kernels do \emph{not} contain $\gamma$. \JuliaMag{} instead carries
$\gamma$ inside the LLG torque; consequently the STT prefactors must be multiplied
by $\gamma$ to sit on the same scale. Omitting this factor leaves the
spin-transfer torque roughly $\gamma \sim 10^{11}$ times too small, and the
current has no visible effect --- a discrepancy we caught precisely because the
standard-problem-5 target below did not move.

\section{Time integration and energy minimization}
\label{sec:solvers}

\JuliaMag{} advances Eq.~\eqref{eq:llg} in two complementary ways.

\paragraph{Time integration.}
The right-hand side $\mathrm{d}\vm/\mathrm{d}t = f(\vm)$ is a single function,
\texttt{llgrhs!}: the Landau--Lifshitz torque of the effective field plus the
spin-transfer torques of the current, if one is set (Sec.~\ref{sec:interface}).
Every integrator and the maximum-torque output evaluate this one function, so a
current-driven simulation can use any of them.

The default integrators are \mumax's explicit solvers, reproduced step for step.
They are Euler, Heun, the Bogacki--Shampine RK23, the classical RK4, and the
Dormand--Prince RK45, the default. Each one follows its \mumax{} counterpart in
the stages, in the renormalization of $\vm$ after every stage, and in the error
estimate: the largest per-cell norm of the embedded error of $\Delta\vm$ (for
RK4, of $(\bm{k}_1-\bm{k}_4)\Delta t$). The two FSAL methods reuse the last stage.
The step-size control is also \mumax's. A step is accepted when the error is
below $\mathrm{MaxErr}$ ($10^{-5}$ by default). The next step is
$\Delta t\cdot\min[2,\max(\tfrac12,0.8\,(\mathrm{MaxErr}/\mathrm{err})^{1/q})]$,
with $q$ the order of the error estimate plus one after an accepted step and
plus two after a rejected one. It is then bounded by the optional
$\Delta t_{\min}$, $\Delta t_{\max}$ and clipped so that no step crosses the end
of the run. A fixed step is also available.

With the same tolerance, a \JuliaMag{} run and a \mumax{} run therefore choose
their steps by the same rule. This makes their step counts comparable, and it
makes the tolerance a shared parameter rather than a source of disagreement
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt}). A finite temperature requires a fixed step: the thermal
field is redrawn every step, and its variance scales as $1/\Delta t$. The
\texttt{Tsit5} method of \texttt{OrdinaryDiffEq.jl}~\cite{diffeq} remains
available as an alternative. Tests check the order of every method on the
analytic damped precession of a macrospin, and check that the adaptive RK45
follows that solution to within its tolerance.

\paragraph{Energy minimization.}
Relaxing to equilibrium by integrating the damped LLG in time is impractical: the
stiff exchange field drives the adaptive step to its floor, so reaching a
nanosecond-scale equilibrium would take of order $10^{12}$ steps. Following
\mumax{}, we relax instead with a dedicated energy minimizer --- a
Barzilai--Borwein steepest-descent method on the unit sphere. Each step moves the
magnetization along the damping torque $\bm{\tau}=-\vm\times(\vm\times\vB)$ with an
exact Cayley rotation
$\vm' = [(4-\tau^2)\vm + 4\Delta t\,\bm{\tau}]/(4+\tau^2)$, $\tau^2=\Delta t^2|\bm{\tau}|^2$,
that preserves $|\vm|=1$ to machine precision. The step size $\Delta t$ is chosen
by the alternating Barzilai--Borwein formulas, and convergence is declared when
the recent per-cell displacement $\max\|\Delta\vm\|$ falls below a threshold. The
total energy of a relaxing film drops monotonically in the windowed sense (the
Barzilai--Borwein step is not strictly monotone step-to-step, as in \mumax{}),
providing an independent convergence check.

\section{Initial states and I/O}
\label{sec:config}

Following \mumax{}, an initial state is a function \texttt{Config: (x,y,z) ->
vector} sampled at each cell centre. \JuliaMag{} provides uniform, vortex,
antivortex, vortex-wall, two-domain, random, and N\'eel- and Bloch-skyrmion
configurations. Localizable textures accept a circulation/charge and a core
polarity, and are placed anywhere with \texttt{translate}, mirroring \mumax's
\texttt{.Transl}. States are read from and written to OVF files (text and binary,
the standard micromagnetic interchange format), so a run can start from a saved
configuration and its snapshots can be visualized in OOMMF, \mumax, or ParaView,
or plotted directly as an out-of-plane colour map with an in-plane vector
overlay.

\section{Geometry and material regions}
\label{sec:regions}

Non-trivial geometries and multi-material samples are built with the same
compositional, function-based style as the initial states. A \emph{shape} is a
predicate \texttt{Shape: (x,y,z) -> Bool}, true inside the sample, in centred
coordinates; primitives (cuboid, cylinder, ellipsoid, cone, superball, layer
slabs) combine through set operations (union, intersection, difference,
complement) and transforms (translate, scale, rotate). Painting a shape with an
integer identifier through \texttt{defregion!} assigns cells to material regions.

Material parameters are then either a single \texttt{Material} or a per-region
\texttt{RegionParams} carrying a lookup table for each parameter
($M_s$, $A$, $\alpha$, $K_u$, the DMI and spin-torque constants). This is where
Julia's multiple dispatch does structural work: every field routine reads its
parameters through accessors (\texttt{msat(params,i,j,k)}, \texttt{aex(...)},
\ldots). For a scalar \texttt{Material} the accessor ignores the cell index and
returns the constant, which the compiler inlines to a plain field read, so a
single-material simulation incurs \emph{no} lookup overhead; for a
\texttt{RegionParams} the same accessor indexes the region table. The field code
is written once and serves both. At a region interface the exchange coupling uses
the harmonic mean of the two stiffnesses (as in \mumax), and cells with
$M_s = 0$ represent unfilled geometry --- they source no field and, once the
magnetization there is cleared, feel no torque --- so a disc or a magnetic
multilayer is expressed by painting material into the desired shape. The
demagnetizing convolution weights each source cell by its own $M_s$, so a
multilayer with layer-dependent magnetization is handled correctly.

\paragraph{Fractional boundary cells.}
A curved boundary on a Cartesian mesh is either a staircase or, with edge
smoothing (\mumax's \texttt{EdgeSmooth}~$=n$), a set of partially filled cells:
each cell is sampled on an $n^3$ sub-grid and stores its filled fraction
$f\in[0,1]$ ($n=0$ gives the staircase, $f\in\{0,1\}$). The fill enters the
terms differently, and we follow \mumax{} except in the thermal field. As a source of the
demagnetizing field, and in every energy sum, a cell carries the moment
$fM_s\vm$ (\mumax's $\bm{M}=\vm M_s\,\mathrm{vol}$), so that a self-interaction
energy is $E = -\tfrac12 V\sum_c f_cM_{s,c}\,\vm_c\cdot\vB_c$ ($V$ the cell
volume; the Zeeman energy has $-1$ in place of $-\tfrac12$). The exchange, DMI and
anisotropy fields, however, are divided by the full region $M_s$, not by $fM_s$ ---
dividing by $fM_s$ would inflate them by $1/f$ in a partial cell, and stiffen the
boundary in the case of the exchange --- and an empty neighbour ($fM_s=0$) is a free
surface: it contributes nothing to the exchange (Neumann), rather than acting as a
neighbour with $\vm=0$. The thermal field uses the moment $fM_s$ of the cell, as the
fluctuation--dissipation theorem requires with these energies; \mumax{} uses $M_s$. The
other rules were checked against \mumax{} field by field, on the disc of
Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt} and, for DMI and anisotropy, on random states. Departures from
them in earlier versions of \JuliaMag{} were found and corrected this way: exchange,
DMI and anisotropy fields divided by $fM_s$ (the last two affect no result reported
here), and a demagnetizing energy whose field ignored the fill. The latter is
guarded by a regression test: a uniformly magnetized cube in which every cell
has fill $f$ must have $f^2$ times the demagnetizing energy of the full cube.

\section{Output, materials, and the simulation interface}
\label{sec:interface}

The user selects what to tabulate from a library of quantities, following
\mumax's data-table model. Besides the time and the spatially averaged
magnetization (globally and per region), the table can record the total and
per-term energies, the maximum torque, the applied field, the topological charge,
and the tracked positions of a vortex core (peak $|m_z|$ with sub-cell
interpolation), a skyrmion (centroid of the topological-charge density), and a
domain wall (zero crossing of the averaged in-plane component). Rows are appended
on a chosen time interval.

A small library of standard materials (Permalloy, Fe, Co, Ni, CoFeB, YIG) returns
representative parameter sets by name. A \texttt{Simulation} object bundles the
geometry, parameters, magnetization, solver settings, and output, and its verbs
are those of a \mumax{} input file. The 40~ns run of Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt}
reads, after the geometry and material are defined:
%
\begin{lstlisting}
sim = togpu(Simulation(mesh, rp; outdir = "disc.out"))
setmag!(sim, VortexConfig(mesh)); relax!(sim)
tableadd!(sim, q_maxtorque())         # TableAdd
tableadd!(sim, q_vortexcore())
tableautosave!(sim, 5e-12)            # TableAutosave
setcurrent!(sim, (0, 0, jz); zhangli = false,
            slonczewski = true, fixedlayer = p)
run!(sim, 40e-9)                      # RK45, MaxErr 1e-5
\end{lstlisting}
%
The table and OVF snapshots are written with \mumax's autosave rule, and every
run logs its wall-clock start and end time, the device, and its step and
evaluation counts. \texttt{togpu} moves the whole simulation to the GPU. Table
columns that have no GPU method, such as the energies, are then evaluated on a
host copy of the state. The solver is chosen by name, e.g.\
\texttt{run!(sim, t; solver = :rk4, fixdt = 5e-14)}.

\section{GPU acceleration through multiple dispatch}
\label{sec:gpu}

The architectural payoff of the design appears at the GPU boundary. Because every
field, torque, and state operation is written against the abstract signature
\texttt{AbstractArray\{T,4\}}, and a CUDA device array \texttt{CuArray\{T,4\}} is
exactly such a type, GPU support is \emph{additive}: it requires neither a
rewrite of the physics nor any change to user code. Moving the magnetization to
the device with \texttt{togpu} causes every subsequent field and torque call to
dispatch, by the array type alone, to a GPU method --- there are no \texttt{if
gpu} branches and no duplicated call sites.

The GPU methods live in a Julia \emph{package extension} that loads only when
\texttt{CUDA.jl} is present, so the core package carries no GPU dependency.
Following \mumax{}, each field and torque term is one CUDA kernel written in
Julia, with one thread per cell, and each kernel repeats the arithmetic of the
corresponding CPU loop line by line. Material parameters enter either as scalars
or, in a multi-region simulation, as per-cell arrays, through the same kernel. The
demagnetizing convolution uses the same real-kernel, batched FFT structure through
\texttt{CUDA.CUFFT}, with the zero padding written once and preserved by the
transforms. A right-hand-side evaluation of the LLG equation then performs no
device allocations and launches $14$ kernels (an earlier version, written with
shifted views and fused broadcasts, launched $182$ and allocated $74$ temporaries
per evaluation, and was launch- and allocation-bound). Reductions (averages,
energies, the minimizer's Barzilai--Borwein step) accumulate in \texttt{Float64}
in either precision. A test suite checks every GPU method against its CPU
counterpart on open and periodic meshes, scalar and region materials, and both DMI
forms. Every field and torque agrees to $3\times10^{-15}$ relative in
\texttt{Float64} and $3\times10^{-7}$ in \texttt{Float32}; $200$ fixed steps of
fourth-order Runge--Kutta agree to $1.4\times10^{-15}$ and $8\times10^{-7}$. The disc validation of Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt} ran entirely on
the GPU.

\paragraph{Performance.}
Table~\ref{tab:perf} compares the cost on the $40$~ns disc run of
Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt} ($64\times64\times8$ cells, zero-padded to
$128\times128\times16$ for the FFTs) on an NVIDIA Quadro P1000. In single precision a \JuliaMag{} evaluation
costs the same as a \mumax{} one, $1.11$~ms. In double precision the demagnetizing
FFTs take $2.8$ of the $3.5$~ms, because this GPU executes \texttt{Float64} at
$1/32$ of the \texttt{Float32} rate. The \texttt{Float32} run reproduces the
\texttt{Float64} one to $3.6\times10^{-6}$ in $\langle\vm\rangle$ over $40$~ns
(Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt}). With the fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta
integration at $50$~fs, \JuliaMag{} needs $3.2\times10^{6}$ evaluations, \mumax's
adaptive fifth-order Runge--Kutta $1.8\times10^{6}$, hence a factor of two in

now its default, makes the same number of evaluations to $0.1\%$ and cuts the
\texttt{Float32} wall time from $4\,008$ to $2\,830$~s. The remaining factor of
$1.4$ is overhead outside the right-hand side. About $230$~s go to the $8\,000$
table rows, whose energies and core position are evaluated on the CPU. The rest,
about $2$~ms per step of six evaluations, is the integrator's own work: the stage
combinations, and two norm reductions per step whose results the host waits for
before it chooses the next step.

\subsection{Standard problem 2}
\label{sec:std2}

The $\mu$MAG standard problem~2~\cite{mumag2} tests static equilibrium states and
the demagnetizing field across a range of particle sizes. A rectangular prism of
aspect ratio $L:d:t = 5:1:0.1$ is saturated along the body diagonal $[1,1,1]$,
the field is removed, and the state is relaxed to remanence. The reduced size is
$d/\ell_{\mathrm{ex}}$, with the magnetostatic exchange length
$\ell_{\mathrm{ex}} = \sqrt{A/K_m}$ and $K_m = \tfrac12\mu_0 M_s^2$; for our
Permalloy parameters $\ell_{\mathrm{ex}} = 5.69$~nm. We relax each size with the
energy minimizer and record the remanent $\langle m_x\rangle$ (along the long
axis) and $\langle m_y\rangle$ (along $d$).

Figure~\ref{fig:std2} compares a sweep of sizes against the \oommf{} reference
table. The agreement tracks the reference across the whole range, from the small
single-domain limit ($\langle m_x\rangle\to 1$, $\langle m_y\rangle\to 0$) to the
large-particle flower state; for example, at $d/\ell_{\mathrm{ex}}=20$ we obtain
$(\langle m_x\rangle,\langle m_y\rangle)=(0.9702,0.0817)$ versus the reference
$(0.9701,0.0819)$. Standard problem~2 thus exercises the demagnetizing field and
the energy minimizer over a wide range of aspect-ratio-driven equilibria.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

  (lines) from the single-domain limit to the flower state.}

irreversible reversal jump. We compute both by ramping the field down from

Figure~\ref{fig:std2hyst} shows $H_c/M_s$ and $H_s/M_s$ against

\subsection{Standard problem 5}
\label{sec:std5}

The $\mu$MAG standard problem~5~\cite{najafi2009} tests the spin-transfer torque.
A $100\times100\times10$~nm Permalloy square ($M_s=8\times10^5$~A/m,
$A=1.3\times10^{-11}$~J/m) is relaxed to a vortex, then an in-plane current
$\bm{j}=(10^{12},0,0)$~A/m$^2$ with polarization $P=1$ and non-adiabaticity
$\xi=0.05$ is applied ($\alpha=0.1$). The current drives the vortex core to a
new equilibrium; the diagnostic is $\langle\vm\rangle$ at 1~ns.

We relax the vortex with the energy minimizer and integrate the current-driven
motion with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta, adding the Zhang--Li torque
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:zhangli} to the LLG right-hand side. Starting from the centred
vortex, $\langle\vm\rangle=(0,0,0.027)$, the core migrates under the current and
settles at
%
\begin{equation}
  \langle\vm\rangle_{\JuliaMag}(1~\mathrm{ns}) = (-0.23488,\ -0.09453,\ 0.02296),
\end{equation}
%
against the \mumax{} reference $(-0.23480,\ -0.09454,\ 0.02296)$: a maximum
component deviation of $9\times10^{-5}$, within \mumax's own $10^{-4}$ tolerance.
Standard problem~5 thus validates the Zhang--Li spin-transfer torque
independently of the field terms exercised by standard problem~4.

\subsection{Current-driven vortex in a disc}
\label{sec:discstt}

The standard problems use rectangular, fully filled meshes. To test a curved
boundary and the Slonczewski torque we compare \JuliaMag{} with \mumax{}
(version~3.11.1) on the free layer of a vortex spin-torque nanopillar: a
Permalloy disc of
diameter $128$~nm and thickness $16$~nm on a $64\times64\times8$ mesh of
$2$~nm cells ($M_s=8\times10^5$~A/m, $A=1.08\times10^{-11}$~J/m,
$\alpha=0.01$), relaxed to a vortex of circulation and polarity $+1$. A current
of $40$~mA flows perpendicular to the disc, $j_z = I/(\pi D^2/4) =
3.108\times10^{12}$~A/m$^2$, polarized by a fixed layer along
$\bm{p}=(\cos20^\circ,\sin20^\circ,0)$ with $P=0.37$, $\Lambda=1$, and
$\epsilon'=0.01$. Both codes run on the GPU, \JuliaMag{} with fourth-order
Runge--Kutta at a fixed step $\Delta t = 5\times10^{-14}$~s and \mumax{} with
its adaptive Dormand--Prince scheme. Unless stated otherwise the boundary is the
staircase (\texttt{EdgeSmooth}~$=0$) of the \mumax{} reference run; the
$40$~ns series at the end of this section uses an edge-smoothed boundary.
$\langle\vm\rangle$ is the average over the disc, each cell weighted by its
fill, as in the \mumax{} table.

\paragraph{Static fields.}
To separate the field terms from the dynamics, we load the vortex relaxed by
\mumax{} into \JuliaMag{} from an OVF file and evaluate the fields on the same
state. The cell fill agrees cell by cell, and the demagnetizing and exchange
fields agree with \mumax's to a maximum relative deviation of
$7.7\times10^{-7}$ and $2.3\times10^{-7}$ --- the single-precision round-off
of \mumax{} --- both for the staircase and for an edge-smoothed boundary
(\texttt{EdgeSmooth}~$=4$, with fractional cells). The energies of the relaxed
vortex agree to all printed digits,
$E_{\mathrm{demag}} = 7.1783\times10^{-19}$~J and
$E_{\mathrm{exch}} = 3.6270\times10^{-18}$~J.

\paragraph{Gyration.}
The current displaces the equilibrium position of the vortex core, and the
core gyrates around it with a slowly decaying amplitude. With $\bm{p}$ in the
film plane this is a damped gyration, not a self-sustained oscillation: the
part of the Slonczewski torque that can compensate the damping of the
gyrotropic mode is set by the out-of-plane component of
$\bm{p}$~\cite{khvalkovskiy2009}, which is zero here. We measure
the frequency from $\langle m_x\rangle(t)$ and $\langle m_y\rangle(t)$ over
$1$--$7$~ns: each signal is resampled onto a uniform time grid (the adaptive
\mumax{} steps are not uniform), its slow drift is removed with a one-period
moving average, and a line is fitted to the unwrapped phase of its analytic
(Hilbert) signal; the frequency is the mean of the $x$ and $y$ estimates.
Table~\ref{tab:discstt} and Fig.~\ref{fig:discstt} compare the two codes for
three cases: the Slonczewski torque alone, the Slonczewski plus Zhang--Li
torques, and free gyration without current (the vortex relaxed under a
$10$~mT in-plane field that is then switched off).

In all three cases the frequencies of the two codes differ by less than
$0.05$~MHz, i.e.\ by less than $10^{-4}$ relative; the spread of
$\pm1.5$~MHz between the $x$ and $y$ estimates bounds the absolute accuracy
of the frequency measurement, which is common to both codes. The free-gyration
amplitude decays at $\Gamma = 0.0797$~ns$^{-1}$ in both. The slightly larger
deviation of $\langle\vm\rangle$ in free gyration comes from its initial state,
which each code relaxes with its own energy minimizer
($\Delta\langle m_x\rangle = 8\times10^{-5}$ at $t=0$). Halving the time step
(tested on the edge-smoothed disc) leaves the frequency unchanged to
$0.01$~MHz and $\langle\vm\rangle$ to below $10^{-7}$, the precision of the
output tables, so the fixed-step integration is converged. The frequency is
measured from $\langle\vm\rangle$. The core position, which \JuliaMag{} locates
with the algorithm of \mumax's \texttt{ext\_corepos} (maximum $|m_z|$ over all
layers, refined by parabolic interpolation), agrees with \mumax's to
$0.006$~nm rms in all three runs, on orbits of radius up to $14$~nm.

\paragraph{The Zhang--Li term with a perpendicular current.}
With $\bm{j} = j_z\hat{\bm{z}}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:zhangli} reduces to
$\bm{h}_s = u\,\partial_z\vm$ with $u = P\mu_Bj_z/(eM_s) = 83$~m/s: it acts only
through the variation of $\vm$ across the thickness. Although that variation is
small, the rate $u/t \approx 5\times10^{9}$~s$^{-1}$ is comparable to the
gyration angular frequency $2\pi f\approx6\times10^{9}$~s$^{-1}$, and the term
lowers the frequency by $4.4\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:discstt}). \mumax{} adds it
whenever a current density is set unless \texttt{DisableZhangLiTorque} is
enabled, so the reference \mumax{} script, written for a spin-valve
nanopillar, applied it by default. The standard micromagnetic model of the
current-perpendicular vortex oscillator retains the Slonczewski torque and the
Oersted field of the current~\cite{khvalkovskiy2009}, and the Zhang--Li term
is not part of it. We therefore take the Slonczewski-only run as the physical
result, and use the Zhang--Li run only to show that both codes implement that
term identically. The comparison is a test of the solver, not a prediction for
a device: neither run includes the Oersted field, which would shift the
frequency of a real oscillator.

This test also illustrates why like-for-like comparisons matter. A first
comparison of \JuliaMag{} (Slonczewski torque only, \texttt{EdgeSmooth}~$=4$)
with the \mumax{} reference (Slonczewski plus the default Zhang--Li torque,
staircase) gave $963.6$~MHz against $930.8$~MHz, a $3.5\%$ gap. The runs
above account for all of it: the Zhang--Li term lowers the reference frequency
from $973.5$ to $930.8$~MHz, and edge smoothing lowers the \JuliaMag{}
frequency from $973.5$ to $963.6$~MHz, and
$(973.5/930.8)\times(963.6/973.5) = 1.035$. The same comparison, carried out
field by field, located and removed the two edge-cell inconsistencies described
in Sec.~\ref{sec:regions}. The \mumax{} inputs, the \JuliaMag{} drivers, and all
time series are distributed with the code.

\paragraph{A 40 ns run with an edge-smoothed boundary.}
A longer comparison follows the dynamics until the gyration has nearly died
out, on the edge-smoothed disc (\texttt{EdgeSmooth}~$=4$, fractional cells
along the rim) and with the Slonczewski torque alone. Both codes log
$\langle\vm\rangle$, the energies, the maximum torque, and the core position
every $5$~ps for $40$~ns (Fig.~\ref{fig:disc40}). The current moves the core
equilibrium to $(-6.35,-1.06)$~nm from the disc centre; the radius of the
orbit around it decays from $6.0$~nm at $2$~ns to $0.31$~nm at $40$~ns, and the
amplitude of $\langle\vm\rangle$ decays at $\Gamma = 0.0806$~ns$^{-1}$ in both
codes. The frequency, $962.3$~MHz over $1$--$40$~ns, drifts from $964.1$ to
$961.9$~MHz as the amplitude decays; in every $2$~ns window the two codes
differ by less than $0.001$~MHz (Fig.~\ref{fig:disc40}b). The largest deviation
of $\langle\vm\rangle$, $4\times10^{-5}$, occurs in the first half
nanosecond, when spin waves launched by the onset of the current vary on the
scale of the output interval; after $10$~ns it is below $10^{-6}$. The total
energy agrees to $2.5\times10^{-5}$ relative, and the core positions to
$0.006$~nm rms.

The maximum torque is the one quantity that does not agree with \mumax{} run
at its default tolerance: it follows \JuliaMag{} until ${\approx}13$~ns and
then levels off near $13$~mT while the \JuliaMag{} value keeps decaying
(Fig.~\ref{fig:disc40}c). The plateau is integration error. Evaluated on the
very same magnetization (the \mumax{} state at $17$~ns, loaded from OVF), the
two codes' torques agree cell by cell to $3\times10^{-5}$ relative. Restarted
from that state, \mumax{} keeps the plateau with its default error tolerance
($\mathrm{MaxErr}=10^{-5}$), but drops within $2$~ps to the \JuliaMag{} value
with $\mathrm{MaxErr}=10^{-7}$ or with fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta;
\JuliaMag{} restarted from the same state does the same. With
$\mathrm{MaxErr}=10^{-7}$ the adaptive step is the same as with the default
tolerance, so the plateau is not a stability limit: the default tolerance
leaves a residual error, of order $10^{-4}$ in $\vm$, in the stiff short-wavelength
exchange modes, which the physical damping would otherwise remove in a few
picoseconds. It is invisible in $\langle\vm\rangle$ and the energies, but sets
the maximum torque once the physical torque has decayed below it. Rerun with
$\mathrm{MaxErr}=10^{-7}$ over the full $40$~ns, \mumax's maximum torque agrees
with \JuliaMag{} to $0.2\%$ rms. A maximum-torque criterion computed by an
adaptive solver should therefore be read with its tolerance in mind.

The same $40$~ns run in \texttt{Float32}, \mumax's precision, reproduces the
\texttt{Float64} \JuliaMag{} run to $3.6\times10^{-6}$ in $\langle\vm\rangle$,
$3\times10^{-6}$ relative in the total energy, and $0.07$~nm in the core
position, with the same frequency ($962.318$~MHz over $1$--$39.5$~ns, from the
orbit phase). Its largest deviations from \mumax{} equal those of the
\texttt{Float64} run to within $5\times10^{-7}$: the comparison with \mumax{} is
limited by the difference between the two time integrations, not by the
floating-point precision.

That difference disappears when \JuliaMag{} integrates with its port of
\mumax's adaptive Dormand--Prince solver (Sec.~\ref{sec:solvers}) at the same
tolerance, $\mathrm{MaxErr}=10^{-5}$. The step-size control then makes
$302\,206$ accepted steps and $1\,813\,249$ evaluations in \texttt{Float32}
($302\,205$ and $1\,813\,243$ in \texttt{Float64}), against \mumax's $302\,912$
and $1\,815\,341$. The maximum torque now levels off exactly as \mumax's does:
over $39$--$40$~ns it averages $12.882$~mT in \texttt{Float32} and $12.880$~mT in
\texttt{Float64}, against $12.882$~mT in \mumax{}. The plateau is thus a property
of the integrator and its tolerance, reproduced by an independent implementation
of the same scheme. The other quantities agree with \mumax{} at least as well
as with the fixed step: the frequency is the same $962.318$~MHz, the largest
deviation of $\langle\vm\rangle$ is $3.4\times10^{-5}$ (rms $2\times10^{-6}$),
the total energy agrees to $1.7\times10^{-5}$ relative, and the core positions
to $0.005$~nm rms.

\subsection{Energy barriers}
\label{sec:barriers}

\paragraph{Method.}
Minimum-energy paths between two relaxed states are found with the geodesic nudged
elastic band (GNEB)~\cite{bessarab2015}: a chain of images, its ends fixed, relaxes
under the component of the energy gradient perpendicular to the path and a spring force
along it, on the product of unit spheres. The tangent is the energy-weighted one of
Ref.~\cite{henkelman2000a}, and the highest image climbs to the saddle
point~\cite{henkelman2000b}. The gradient is the effective field, so the energy that the
band follows is consistent to rounding with the field that drives it; a test checks
this for every term, and a warning is printed where it does not hold (exchange or DMI in
partially filled cells). The whole chain is relaxed with the Barzilai--Borwein steps and
Cayley rotations of the minimizer. Forces and the spring constant are expressed in
tesla, and the images can be on the GPU.

A climbing image that has converged is a stationary point, not necessarily a transition
state. We therefore compute the lowest eigenvalues of the second variation of the energy,
\begin{equation}
  \mathsf{H}\,\delta\vm = \mathsf{P}\bigl[(\vm\cdot\vB_{\mathrm{eff}})\,\delta\vm
  - \vB_{\mathrm{lin}}(\delta\vm)\bigr],
  \label{eq:hessian}
\end{equation}
where $\vB_{\mathrm{lin}}$ is the linear part of the field (all terms but Zeeman) and
$\mathsf{P}$ the projection on the tangent planes of $\vm$. In the $M_s$-weighted inner
product $\mathsf{H}$ is symmetric; its eigenvalues are curvatures, in tesla. The number of
negative ones, the index, is 0 at a minimum and 1 at a first-order saddle. The operator is
applied with one field evaluation and diagonalized as a dense matrix, so that no negative
eigenvalue can be missed, or with a matrix-free solver for larger systems.

\paragraph{Macrospin.}
For the Stoner--Wohlfarth particle~\cite{stoner1948} in a field $h=M_sB/2K$ opposite to the
magnetization the barrier is $KV(1-h)^2$, reproduced to $10^{-14}$ for $h=0$--$0.9$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gneb}(a)). The curvatures at the saddle are $-2K(1-h^2)/M_s$, reproduced
to $10^{-9}$, and zero for the rotation about the axis.

\paragraph{Reversal of an ellipse.}
The second case is that of the string-method demonstration of magnum.np: a
$470~\mathrm{nm}\times170~\mathrm{nm}\times4~\mathrm{nm}$ ellipse of Permalloy on 5~nm
cells, reversed from $+x$ to $-x$. Two initial paths are used. In the first, the only
intermediate state is uniform along $+y$, and the path keeps the inversion symmetry
$\vm(\bm{r})=\vm(-\bm{r})$. In the second, a wall sweeps across the element. With 21 images
the barriers are $2.414\times10^{-18}$~J and $1.710\times10^{-18}$~J
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gneb}(b)). They do not depend on the number of images (11, 21, 31) or on the
spring constant (0.3, 1, 3~T). MicroMagnetic.jl~\cite{wang2024}, given the same end states
and the same initial path, obtains barriers larger by $2.0\times10^{-4}$ and
$1.7\times10^{-4}$; the same differences appear when one image is evaluated in both codes,
so they come from the two demagnetizing kernels. The curvatures settle which path is
physical. The saddle of the wall path has one negative eigenvalue,
$-2.07$~mT, and the next is $+2.80$~mT. The saddle of the uniform path has two, $-15.9$~mT
and $-5.4$~mT. Under inversion the first of these modes is even and the second odd, so a
band that keeps the symmetry cannot leave the second: the uniform path is not a
transition path.

\paragraph{Skyrmion collapse.}
The third case is a single Bloch skyrmion in a film periodic in the plane, with bulk DMI
($60\times60$ cells of 2~nm, $M_s=3.84\times10^5$~A/m, $A=3.25\times10^{-12}$~J/m,
$D=5.83\times10^{-4}$~J/m$^2$, $B=0.12$~T; the open DMI boundary in $z$, as in
MicroMagnetic.jl). The initial state has topological charge $-1$. The barrier to the
uniform state is $7.94228\times10^{-20}$~J, equal in the two codes to $5.5\times10^{-13}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gneb}(c)), and independent of the spring constant and of 21 or 31 images to
$10^{-11}$. With 11 images the band does not converge. The saddle has exactly one negative
curvature, $-2.90$~T, the next being $+0.110$~T. The starting state is a minimum up to
two zero modes ($10^{-13}$~T), the translations. A lattice of four
skyrmions in the same film, by contrast, relaxes to a state that is not a minimum: it has
two negative curvatures of $-1.6$~mT, so that the minimizer stopped on a
symmetric stationary point, and the barrier found from it, although both codes agree on it
to $9\times10^{-9}$, is not that of a metastable state (the index of the top image is 4).
The curvature test is what reveals this.

\subsection{Linear eigenmodes}
\label{sec:eigen}

\paragraph{Method.}
About an equilibrium $\vm_0$ the undamped LLG equation linearizes to
$\mathrm{d}\,\delta\vm/\mathrm{d}t=\gamma\,\vm_0\times\mathsf{H}\,\delta\vm$ with the operator of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hessian}~\cite{daquino2009}. In the variable $u=\sqrt{M_s}\,\delta\vm$ the operator
becomes $\gamma\mathsf{J}\mathsf{A}$, with $\mathsf{A}$ symmetric and
$\mathsf{J}u=\vm_0\times u$, and at a minimum its eigenvalues are $\pm i\omega$. Small systems are
solved as a dense Hermitian problem through the Cholesky factor of $\mathsf{A}$. For larger ones,
$\mathsf{A}^{-1}\mathsf{J}$ is antisymmetric in the inner product $(x,\mathsf{A}y)$, and a Lanczos
recurrence in that product gives the lowest frequencies first; each step solves
$\mathsf{A}w=v$ by conjugate gradients, with one field evaluation per iteration, on the CPU or
the GPU. Further passes, kept orthogonal to the modes found, recover degenerate
frequencies. The decay rates are those of first order in $\alpha$. A state that is not a
minimum, or that has a zero mode (a translation, a rotation), is reported as such.

\paragraph{Analytic cases.}
The Kittel frequency of a macrospin, and the spin waves of a periodic chain with
the dispersion of the discretized exchange and the non-reciprocal shift
$\pm(2D/M_s)\sin(kc)/c$ of the interfacial DMI, are reproduced to $10^{-9}$ or better, and
the modes returned satisfy the linearized right-hand side of the equation of motion itself,
also with regions of different $M_s$.

\paragraph{FMR standard problem.}
The problem of Baker \textit{et al.}~\cite{baker2017} is a $120\times120\times10$~nm Permalloy film in an
in-plane field of 80~kA/m at $35^\circ$. Reference~\cite{baker2017} gives the fifteen lowest
frequencies and damping times ($\alpha=0.008$) from an eigenvalue solver on 5~nm cells. With that
mesh, and the frequencies scaled to the gyromagnetic ratio of the reference
($2.210173\times10^5$~m/(A\,s)), \JuliaMag{} agrees with all fifteen frequencies to
$7\times10^{-4}$ and with the damping times to $1.2\times10^{-3}$. The differences are
systematic, and we traced them. They shrink as the accuracy of \JuliaMag's demagnetizing
kernel, which follows \mumax, is increased; with the analytic tensor of
Newell \textit{et al.}~\cite{newell1993} the frequencies differ from the table by a common factor,
$1.00031$, and after removing that single factor all fifteen frequencies agree within the rounding of
the table (0.5~MHz) and the damping times to 0.6~ps. We have not identified the
origin of the factor; it is uniform, as a difference in the gyromagnetic ratio would be. The
matrix-free solver gives the same frequencies as the dense one to $4\times10^{-15}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:eigen}(a) compares the frequencies with the ringdown spectrum computed with
\oommf{} in Ref.~\cite{baker2017}.

\paragraph{Gyrotropic mode of a vortex.}
For the disc of Sec.~\ref{sec:discstt} (staircase boundary, $64\times64\times8$ cells, 25\,824
magnetic) the lowest mode is the gyrotropic one, $f=968.234$~MHz with a decay rate
$\Gamma=0.07940$~ns$^{-1}$; the next modes lie above 10~GHz. We compare it with the
equation of motion, integrated from the vortex displaced by fields of 10, 5, 2 and 1~mT
(double precision, fourth-order Runge--Kutta). The frequency, from the phase of
$\langle m_x\rangle+i\langle m_y\rangle$ over 2--8~ns, increases linearly with the square of the
amplitude, and extrapolates to $968.088$~MHz at zero
(Fig.~\ref{fig:eigen}(c)). The difference from the eigenfrequency, $-1.5\times10^{-4}$, is the shift
of second order in $\alpha$: the exact eigenvalues of the damped operator of two coarser discs
give a relative shift of $-1.68\,\alpha^2$ and $-1.69\,\alpha^2$, that is, $968.072$~MHz for the
damped mode, and the extrapolation agrees with it to $2\times10^{-5}$. The extrapolated decay
rate, $0.0794$~ns$^{-1}$, agrees with the eigenmode to $2\times10^{-4}$. (The estimator
used for Table~\ref{tab:discstt}, a moving average followed by a Hilbert transform, is biased by
$+0.9$~MHz on a signal that decays at this rate. The bias is the same for both
codes, so it does not affect their comparison, but the $969.8$~MHz of that table is
$0.1\%$ above the frequency of the gyration.)

\section{Applications}
\label{sec:applications}

Beyond the quantitative benchmarks, two short studies illustrate the kind of
problem \JuliaMag{} is meant for: nonuniform equilibria on non-rectangular
geometry, and current-driven topological textures on a periodic wire. Both are
driven entirely through the user-facing simulation interface of
Section~\ref{sec:interface}.

\subsection{Vortex hysteresis in a disc}
\label{sec:vortex}

A magnetic vortex in a thin Permalloy disc traces a characteristic hysteresis
loop under an in-plane field~\cite{guslienko2002}. We take a
$500~\mathrm{nm}\times20~\mathrm{nm}$ disc, painted as a cylinder on a
$100\times100\times4$ mesh with the cells outside the disc left empty
($M_s=0$; Section~\ref{sec:regions}), and sweep the field along $x$ over
$\pm150$~mT, relaxing the magnetization with the energy minimizer at each step
and continuing from the previous state. Figure~\ref{fig:vortex} shows the result.
Here $\langle\vm\rangle$ is the average over the disc's cells (the empty cells
excluded, as in mumax$^3$'s table); an average over the whole rectangular box would
be diluted by the disc's area fraction $\approx\pi/4$. At zero field the centred
vortex gives $\langle m_x\rangle=0$. The field displaces the core along a nearly
linear, reversible branch until the vortex is expelled between 55 and 60~mT, and
the disc jumps to its nearly saturated branch ($\langle m_x\rangle=0.994$ at
150~mT). The disc must be wide and thin for this reversible core-displacement
branch to appear --- the signature that distinguishes a vortex loop from a square
one --- which is exactly the regime where an accurate demagnetizing field
matters most.

On the return sweep the quasi-uniform state persists through zero field
(remanence $\langle m_x\rangle=0.961$) and loses stability at $-15$~mT. Which
state replaces it is not fixed by the energy landscape alone. The loss of
stability is a bifurcation, and the state the minimizer reaches depends on
arbitrarily small asymmetries.

The problem is symmetric under the mirror $y\to-y$, and \JuliaMag{} preserves
that symmetry exactly: the state at $-10$~mT is mirror-symmetric to
$3\times10^{-6}$. Along this symmetric path the disc reverses directly to the
opposite quasi-uniform state at $-15$~mT. On the ascending branch of the same
run, rounding errors break the symmetry and a vortex renucleates at $+15$~mT.

A random perturbation of amplitude $10^{-6}$ added to $\vm$ before each
relaxation (dashed curves) makes the outcome depend on the random seed. In one
realization the vortex renucleates at zero field; in another the disc reverses
at $-10$~mT. Tilting the field by $0.1^\circ$ or $1^\circ$ from $x$ gives yet other
switching fields, $-10$ and $-20$~mT. An earlier version of the code, in which the odd
components of the demagnetizing kernel were not yet set exactly to zero on their
symmetry planes (Sec.~\ref{sec:demag}), renucleated the vortex at $-15$~mT.

The reversible branch and the annihilation field are the same in every variant.
The renucleation field, however, is set by the thermal fluctuations or the
imperfections of a real disc, so the zero-temperature quasi-static loop
resolves the competing states but not which one a sample selects.

\paragraph{Finite temperature.}
To let thermal fluctuations select the state, instead of rounding errors, we
repeated the loop with the stochastic LLG equation at $50$ and $300$~K, with eight
independent realizations at each temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:vortexT}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Thermal field.} Brown's thermal field~\cite{brown1963}, integrated
  with the Heun scheme at a fixed step of $0.1$~ps, in single precision on the
  GPU. In an ensemble of non-interacting macrospins the same field reproduces the
  Langevin function, $\langle m_x\rangle=L(\xi)$ for $\xi=1$--$20$, within the
  sampling error (at most $0.018$, about $2\sigma$, over two independent ensembles).
  \item \emph{Protocol.} At each $5$~mT step the dynamics runs for $2$~ns, with
  the damping raised to $\alpha=0.5$; $\alpha$ sets the relaxation rate, not the
  equilibrium. $\langle\vm\rangle$ is averaged over the last quarter of each step.
  \item \emph{Convergence.} On the virgin branch at $50$~K this protocol
  reproduces the zero-temperature reversible branch within the thermal noise.
  Dwells of $0.5$ and $1$~ns lag behind it and delay the annihilation by one field
  step. A step of $0.05$~ps gives the same branch within the noise at both
  temperatures.
  \item \emph{Caveats.} $M_s$ and $A$ keep their room-temperature values. The
  sweep rate, $2.5\times10^{6}$~T/s, exceeds experimental rates by many orders of
  magnitude, so the switching fields below are upper bounds for thermally
  activated switching.
\end{itemize}

The virgin branch and the annihilation field do not change: the vortex is expelled
at $61\pm2$~mT at $50$~K and at $60$~mT in every $300$~K realization. Spin waves
lower the saturated $\langle m_x\rangle$ by $0.0013$ at $50$~K and $0.0076$ at
$300$~K. The ratio, $6.1$, matches the ratio of the temperatures, as expected for
a thermal reduction linear in $T$.

The return branches change in kind. In 28 of the 32 branches (16 loops, two
branches each), $\langle m_y\rangle$ grows to $0.15$--$0.48$ before the switch:
the fluctuations break the mirror symmetry and the disc relaxes into a C-shaped
state, which the symmetric zero-temperature calculation cannot reach. Its
remanence is $\langle m_x\rangle=0.70$--$0.86$, against $0.961$ at $T=0$.

Every one of the 32 switches then goes through a vortex. The direct reversal of
the deterministic descending branch never occurs. The switching fields are
$\mp15.0\pm3.8$~mT on the descending and ascending branches at $50$~K, and
$-13.1\pm5.9$ and $+11.9\pm4.6$~mT at $300$~K (mean and standard deviation over
realizations, at a field resolution of $5$~mT). The difference between the two
temperatures is within the statistical uncertainty of eight realizations.

The nucleated vortex falls into one of two classes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In 21 cases, $\langle m_x\rangle$ joins the reversible branch of the
  virgin curve, and the vortex is annihilated at $60$--$65$~mT, as on the virgin
  branch.
  \item In the other 11, $\langle m_x\rangle$ just after nucleation lies well above
  that branch ($0.69$--$0.73$ at $20$~mT, against $0.44$), and the vortex state
  is lost already at $25$--$35$~mT.
\end{itemize}
All 11 short-lived vortices come from a C state, but C states also lead to the
first class (17 cases), so the precursor alone does not decide the outcome.

The GPU random stream is deterministic, so one loop of each class (50~K, seeds 1
and 7) was re-run with $\vm$ snapshots every $0.1$~ns and the core position
every $10$~ps, from $0$ to $-30$~mT. The re-runs reproduce the stored loops
bit for bit. Figure~\ref{fig:vortex_snap} shows the two classes.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The short-lived class is a two-vortex state.} Two cores of equal
  polarity enter at opposite edges at $-15$~mT and move along point-symmetric
  tracks. By the end of the $-20$~mT step they sit near the top and bottom edges
  ($r/R\approx0.85$), with the central domain along the field, hence the large
  $|\langle m_x\rangle|$. They leave one after the other at $-25$~mT, $0.5$~ns
  apart.
  \item \emph{The long-lived class is a single vortex.} It enters at the edge,
  crosses the disc, and settles on the displacement branch: the core sits
  perpendicular to the field and moves out at about $4$~nm/mT, from
  $r/R=0.49$ at $-20$~mT to $0.64$ at $-30$~mT. It is annihilated at $-60$~mT.
  \item \emph{The single vortex reverses its polarity on the way in.} In the
  snapshot $0.7$~ns into the $-15$~mT step a dip of opposite polarity lies
  $30$~nm from the moving core, and $0.2$~ns later only a core of the opposite
  polarity is left. This sequence is the one expected for switching through a
  vortex--antivortex pair~\cite{vanwaeyenberge2006,hertel2007}, although the
  $0.1$~ns snapshots do not resolve the antivortex itself. The core moves at
  about $240$~m/s before the reversal ($180$--$310$~m/s in $50$~ps secants),
  below the critical velocity $330\pm37$~m/s reported for
  Permalloy~\cite{lee2008}, so that criterion alone does not account for it.
\end{itemize}
The narrow spread of $\langle m_x\rangle$ in the short-lived class
($0.69$--$0.73$ at $20$~mT) suggests that all 11 of its loops reach this same
two-vortex state, but only one of them was imaged.

\subsection{Current-driven skyrmion on an infinite wire}
\label{sec:skyrmion}

A N\'eel skyrmion stabilized by interfacial DMI and perpendicular anisotropy is
driven along a stripe by a Zhang--Li spin current. To model an infinite wire we
make the mesh periodic along $x$ ($\mathtt{pbc}=(1,0,0)$), so the skyrmion
re-enters from the far end rather than being pinned or annihilated at an edge; the
exchange, DMI, demagnetizing, and spin-torque terms all honour the periodic axis.
On a $300~\mathrm{nm}\times200~\mathrm{nm}\times1~\mathrm{nm}$ stripe
($M_s=5.8\times10^5$~A/m, $A=1.5\times10^{-11}$~J/m, $K_u=8\times10^5$~J/m$^3$,
$D=3.0\times10^{-3}$~J/m$^2$, $P=1$, $\xi=0.2$) a current
$\bm{j}=(2\times10^{12},0,0)$~A/m$^2$ drives the skyrmion against the electron
flow at a steady $v_x\approx-178$~m/s, with a transverse skyrmion-Hall deflection
along $y$. Figure~\ref{fig:skyrmion} shows the drift: the recorded position is
periodic, so it is unwrapped before fitting the velocity, giving a straight
$x(t)$ across the wrap-around re-entry, and the snapshots confirm the
hedgehog texture is preserved. The skyrmion position is obtained as the centroid
of the topological-charge density, computed circularly along the periodic axis so
that it remains correct when the texture straddles the wrap seam.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

\section{Conclusion and outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented \JuliaMag, a micromagnetic solver written entirely in Julia,
and shown that a transparent implementation --- one in which each field, torque,
and solver is expressed directly at the level of the underlying mathematics ---
can reproduce the established \oommf{} and \mumax{} reference codes to high
accuracy. On the $\mu$MAG standard problems the spatially averaged magnetization
agrees with the references to $\sim 4\times10^{-3}$ (dynamic reversal, standard
problem~4), to $\sim 10^{-3}$ in the remanence and coercive field across a wide
range of aspect ratios (static equilibria, standard problem~2), and to
$9\times10^{-5}$ for the current-driven vortex (spin-transfer torque, standard
problem~5). On a current-driven vortex in a disc, run on the GPU, the
fields agree with \mumax{} cell by cell to $10^{-6}$ and the gyration frequency
to better than $10^{-4}$, and $\langle\vm\rangle$ to $4\times10^{-5}$ over
$40$~ns, once both codes apply the same torques to the same boundary; that
comparison also showed that \mumax{} adds the Zhang--Li torque to a
current-perpendicular simulation by default, and that its maximum-torque output
at the default tolerance reflects integration error late in a long run.
Beyond the benchmarks, the same interface handles a vortex hysteresis
loop on a non-rectangular disc, at zero and at finite temperature, and a
current-driven skyrmion on a periodic infinite wire
(Section~\ref{sec:applications}). At zero temperature the renucleation of the
vortex is a symmetry-breaking bifurcation, selected by round-off. At finite
temperature the fluctuations select it: the disc always passes through a vortex,
usually after a C-shaped precursor. Three architectural choices carry
the design: a
component-first $(3,N_x,N_y,N_z)$ data layout, multiple dispatch on an abstract
array type, and a single $(x,y,z)$ index convention with the origin at the sample
centre. Together they keep the source small and, crucially, made GPU support
a purely additive step: because every routine already operates on
\texttt{AbstractArray\{T,4\}}, device execution required only that the arrays be
\texttt{CuArray}s and that a handful of inner kernels acquire GPU methods, with no
change to the call sites (Section~\ref{sec:gpu}), following the path that took
\mumax{} to the GPU~\cite{lopezdiaz2012}. With one kernel per term, as in \mumax{},
a single-precision evaluation of the equation of motion costs the same as in
\mumax{} on the same device, and a single-precision run reproduces the
double-precision one to $4\times10^{-6}$.

Beyond conventional acceleration, a transparent, dispatch-based, and fully
differentiable-by-construction Julia implementation is a natural substrate for
the machine-learning methods now reshaping computational micromagnetics. Two
directions are especially promising. First, the magnetostatic field --- the
dominant cost --- is increasingly being approximated or accelerated by neural
surrogates: physics-informed and physics-aware machine-learning schemes have been
applied to stray-field computation and to micromagnetic energy minimization,
replacing or preconditioning the expensive convolution and its associated
minimization~\cite{schaffer2023,schaffer2025,schaffer2022}. A minimizer and demag
solver expressed as ordinary Julia functions can be differentiated and composed
with such surrogates directly, without a foreign-function boundary. Second, the
LLG dynamics themselves can be learned or extrapolated: neural ordinary
differential equations have been used to forecast the outcome of spintronic
experiments~\cite{chen2022}, and the same \texttt{DifferentialEquations.jl}
ecosystem~\cite{diffeq} that supplies \JuliaMag's \texttt{Tsit5} integrator also provides
the adjoint-sensitivity and neural-ODE machinery on which such approaches are
built. Positioning \JuliaMag{} within this ecosystem --- rather than as an
isolated, hand-optimized binary --- is, in our view, its most significant
long-term advantage.

Energy barriers and linear eigenmodes (Secs.~\ref{sec:barriers} and~\ref{sec:eigen})
are the most recent additions. A script front end in the style of \mumax{} is next; multi-backend
GPU execution through \texttt{KernelAbstractions.jl} remains planned. The code is open source and available at
\url{https://github.com/rlnovak/juliamag}.
